01

The concentration trap

Most portfolios do not fail because the investor picked the wrong asset. They fail because the investor picked too few of them — and the few they picked all shared the same hidden risk.

Ask a typical investor where their money sits and you will hear a familiar pattern: domestic stocks, the employer’s shares, maybe a hot sector fund bought near its peak. Behavioural economists call the first habit home-country bias — the persistent overweighting of one’s own market, even though no single country dominates global returns for long. The second habit is loyalty mistaken for strategy: employees who hold their company’s stock watched that logic detonate at Enron, where workers lost both their jobs and their retirement savings in the same bankruptcy.

Harry Markowitz, who formalised modern portfolio theory in 1952, called diversification “the only free lunch in investing.” The logic is arithmetic, not opinion: combine assets whose prices do not move in lockstep and the portfolio’s volatility falls without necessarily sacrificing expected return. The lunch is only free, however, if the assets are genuinely different — different sectors, different geographies, different economic drivers. Ten tech stocks are not diversification. They are a concentrated bet wearing a costume.

02

Geography is a risk factor

A portfolio can be perfectly balanced across sectors and still hinge on a single strait, a single island, or a single refinery town. The map itself is a position.

Single point of failure

Taiwan makes the world’s intelligence

Roughly 90% of the most advanced semiconductors on earth are manufactured in Taiwan, and one company — TSMC — captures about 70% of global foundry revenue, rising to 73% in Q2 2026 on AI demand (Counterpoint Research). Nvidia’s AI accelerators, Apple’s processors, AMD’s chips: virtually all of them pass through fabs on a single island. A cross-strait crisis would not merely dent tech stocks — it would halt production of phones, cars, servers and weapons systems worldwide.

  • TSMC’s Arizona, Kumamoto and Dresden fabs will not reach frontier-node scale before 2028–2030 at the earliest.
  • For the rest of this decade, Taiwan remains the world’s chokepoint for advanced AI chips.
Strained artery

Energy still travels through straits

As the companion brief When Wars Move Markets traced, a large share of the world’s traded oil and liquefied natural gas squeezes through a handful of maritime chokepoints — Hormuz, Malacca, Bab el-Mandeb. Any portfolio or any economy indexed to energy prices is, whether it knows it or not, indexed to the politics of those straits.

Latent leverage

Refining is the quiet monopoly

Mining gets the headlines; refining gets the leverage. China dominates the refining of rare earths and much of the world’s battery-chemical processing — the unglamorous middle of the clean-energy supply chain. Export controls there would not stop mines from digging; they would stop everyone else from building. Diversification that ignores the middle of the chain is decoration.

The personal-finance translation is direct: if every holding in a portfolio ultimately depends on the same geography — the same strait, the same island, the same trade relationship — the portfolio is concentrated no matter how many tickers it contains.

03

Sectors rotate; survivors diversify

Every decade anoints a sector that “can’t lose” — and then teaches the holders otherwise.

In 2000 it was technology: the Nasdaq fell roughly three-quarters from peak to trough, and investors who were “diversified” across twenty dot-com names discovered they owned one risk. In 2008 it was financials, where the sector at the centre of the crisis led the market down. In 2022 the wheel turned again: energy stocks surged while technology sank — the year’s winners and losers were almost a mirror image of the previous decade’s.

That rotation is the whole argument. No one can reliably predict which sector leads next, but a portfolio spread across sectors does not need to: it harvests whichever rotation arrives. Sector concentration is a forecast; sector diversification is the admission that forecasts fail — and a structure that survives the failure.

04

When nations don’t diversify

Everything above applies to countries too — with the stakes multiplied by millions of livelihoods. A nation whose economy is one commodity is a household with one income and no savings.

The warning

Venezuela: the concentrated nation

For decades oil accounted for the overwhelming majority of Venezuela’s export revenue and government income. When crude prices collapsed in the mid-2010s, the economy had no second engine: output, imports, the currency and living standards fell together. Economists call the broader pattern Dutch disease — a dominant resource sector hollows out everything else, leaving the country maximally exposed to the one price it cannot control.

The model

Norway: oil wealth, converted

Norway also found oil. Instead of spending the windfall, it funnelled surplus revenues into the Government Pension Fund Global — now roughly $2.2 trillion, the largest sovereign wealth fund on earth (Norges Bank, end-2025). The fund owns about 1.5% of every listed company in the world: more than 7,000 companies across 60 countries (13F Insight, Q4 2025). Norway took the most concentrated asset imaginable — hydrocarbons under one seabed — and converted it into the most diversified portfolio ever assembled. A fiscal rule caps annual spending at 3% of expected real returns, so today’s voters cannot consume tomorrow’s capital.

  • 2025 return: 15.1%, about $247 billion — driven by global tech, financial and materials stocks.
  • The Gulf states are running the same playbook: Saudi Arabia’s PIF (~$0.9tn) targets $2.67tn by 2030 to fund economic transformation.

The lesson generalises: diversification is not just a portfolio tactic but a development strategy. Countries that recycle commodity booms into broad-based assets — financial, industrial, human — buy insurance against the bust. Countries that don’t, buy the bust.

05

The playbook

Diversification is a discipline, not a product. Here is what it looks like in practice — at household scale and at national scale.

For individuals

Build the buffer

  • Go global: a world-equity index fund is the simplest cure for home-country bias.
  • Cap any single position: no stock, sector or country should be able to sink you alone.
  • Rebalance on a schedule: sell a little of what soared, buy a little of what lagged — annually is enough.
  • Audit hidden concentrations: employer stock plus a domestic index can mean triple exposure to one economy.
  • Keep the emergency fund boring: diversification starts with cash that never needs to be sold in a panic.
For governments

Insure the economy

  • Sovereign wealth funds: convert finite resource revenues into permanent, diversified financial wealth (the Norway model).
  • Industrial policy with an exit: build second and third engines — manufacturing, services, tech — before the first one stalls.
  • Friend-shoring & redundancy: dual-source critical inputs; no single strait, island or supplier should be load-bearing.
  • Strategic reserves: stockpiles of energy, food and critical minerals buy time when diversification fails.
  • Fiscal rules: cap windfall spending so booms build buffers instead of appetites.
06

Where the argument bends

Open questions & honest limits — read these before citing

  • Correlations go to one in a crisis. Diversification’s protection is weakest exactly when it is most wanted: in panics, nearly everything falls together. The buffer softens drawdowns; it does not abolish them.
  • Diworsification is real. Past a point, adding holdings adds fees, complexity and overlap without adding safety — fifty funds that all own the same mega-caps are one fund with extra paperwork.
  • Don’t diversify into what you don’t understand. Exotic assets bought for their low correlation can carry risks — illiquidity, fraud, leverage — that swamp the diversification benefit.
  • Geography cuts both ways. Spreading into unstable jurisdictions can import political risk faster than it exports concentration risk. Diversify across stable, uncorrelated exposures, not merely across borders.
  • Figures are as-reported. Foundry shares come from Counterpoint Research via trade press; sovereign-fund sizes for non-Norwegian funds are analyst estimates, not audited figures. Norway’s numbers are the exception — published and audited.
