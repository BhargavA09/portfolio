The AI Agent Economy

AI has crossed from answering questions to doing work — using tools, keeping memory, and iterating in planning loops. Here is what that changes about who does what, who gets paid, and who carries the risk.

01

From answers to actions

For several years the story of AI in knowledge work was about answers: you asked a question, a model produced text, and the exchange ended at the reply. What is happening now is categorically different, and it matters more than the models’ growing eloquence — AI systems are starting to do things.

The chatbot’s question was “what do you know?” The agent’s question is “what can you touch?” Three changes answer it.

The decisive change

Tool use: from language to leverage

A model that can call an API can alter the world: query a database, move money, open a ticket, commit code. Language was the interface to human attention; tools are the interface to human systems. This is what converts a clever reply into an economic act — the boundary that matters is no longer what the model can say but what it is permitted to do.

The compounding change

Memory: from sessions to continuity

A chatbot starts every conversation as a stranger. An agent with persistent memory does not: it knows what was tried, what failed, and what is still pending. That continuity turns a sequence of one-shot answers into a continuing task. Organisations hire for responsibility over time, not single utterances, and memory is the cheapest way to give a system something like responsibility.

The reliability change

Planning loops: errors become corrections

Propose a plan, act, observe the result, replan. The loop buys the property that moved agents from demo to utility: recovery from their own mistakes without a human re-prompting. Reliability comes from the loop, not from a flawless model.

No fundamentally new intelligence was required — only the scaffolding around the model maturing. The next gains will come as much from better environments to act in as from smarter models.

02

Where agents already do real work

Strip away the marketing and a pattern emerges: agents succeed first wherever the task is legible, the feedback is fast, and the cost of a mistake is bounded. That is why three domains lead.

Legible task · fast feedback

Code: the agent’s home turf

The work is expressed in a formal language, and — crucially — the agent can see its own failures: the compiler complains, the tests fail. An agent that writes code and runs the test suite closes its own feedback loop in seconds. Programming is one of the few knowledge tasks where correctness can be checked automatically — and the rule generalises: the further a task is from a fast, cheap verifier, the slower agent adoption will be.

High volume · structured input

Support: the tier that never sleeps

Support is a queue of structured problems with clear resolution criteria. Agents triage and resolve the routine tier while escalating the ambiguous cases — but they are only as good as the knowledge base they consult, and they fail where it is stale.

Rule-bound · auditable

Ops: the quiet takeover

Reconciliation, data entry, report generation — the unglamorous machinery of every company, rule-bound and auditable: an agent does it at volume, a human spot-checks afterwards. This is where the most value accrues least visibly.

An agent succeeds wherever the task is legible, the feedback is fast, and the cost of a mistake is bounded.

— the deployment rule, stated three ways across code, support, and ops

The pattern tells you what to watch next: whichever field builds a fast, cheap verifier becomes the next one where agents graduate from assistant to worker.

03

The new economics of doing things

Every economy is a system of bottlenecks, and agents move the bottleneck. The cost of executing a well-specified task is collapsing toward the cost of compute; the cost of specifying it well — and of trusting the result — is not.

Where the scarcity moved
ResourceOld economyAgent economy
ExecutionScarce — human hours, linear and expensiveCheap — parallel, marginal cost near compute
SpecificationCheap — brief a person and they fill the gapsScarce — vague goals produce confident wrong work
VerificationBundled — the worker checks as they goUnbundled — someone must check the agent’s work
LiabilityClear — the employee or the vendor is responsibleMurky — the agent acted, but who authorised it?

Qualitative, not measured. The claim is about the direction of the shift, which is structural, not its speed, which is not.

Trust is the new throughput

Labour was expensive and trust was cheap — interviews, references, probation. With agents it is the reverse: where checking is cheaper than doing, agents scale freely; where checking costs as much as doing, or a mistake is catastrophic, agents stay fenced. The adoption frontier is set not by model quality but by verification economics.

Liability is the hidden tax

When an employee errs, the liability chain is settled law. When an agent acting under delegated permission charges the wrong customer or deletes the wrong records, the chain is foggy: the vendor sold the model, the deployer configured it, the user prompted it. Unclear liability taxes every deployment — and it will be the last bottleneck cleared, because it is cleared by courts and contracts, not benchmarks.

The net effect: the price of getting something done falls, while the price of being sure it was done right rises in relative importance. Margin migrates toward whoever sells certainty — verification, audit trails, insurance — and away from whoever merely sells execution.

04

What this means for builders

If execution is cheap, scarcity moves up the stack: deciding what is worth doing, and knowing whether it was done well. The skill that compounds is no longer typing — it is judgment.

Taste becomes the job

When a hundred candidate implementations can be generated in an afternoon, the value is not in producing them but in choosing: which design is maintainable, which edge case matters, which abstraction survives the next requirement. The ability to look at machine-generated output and spot the flaw that matters becomes the core skill, trained only by having built things the slow way first. There is no shortcut to taste.

Design for delegation

The second shift is in what we build. Products designed for humans — buttons, dashboards, forms — are awkward for agents. Products designed for delegation look different: deterministic identifiers, machine-readable contracts, structured outputs, APIs first. The winning pattern is the checkpoint architecture: free running through reversible steps, a check before every irreversible one.

Build this in

Agent-shaped habits

  • Reversible by default: destructive actions require an explicit grant, not an implicit assumption.
  • Scorecards over vibes: an agent in production needs a tracked success rate.
Watch for this

The failure modes

  • Confident wrongness: agents fail at the top of their competence — looking right is worse than looking wrong.
  • Skill atrophy: teams that stop doing the work stop knowing when the agent is wrong. Keep humans practised on the hard parts.
05

What this means for investors

Capital follows margin, and margin follows the bottleneck. In the agent economy the bottleneck is not intelligence — intelligence is commoditising — it is everything around it.

Where the margin pools

The durable layers

  • Distribution and workflow ownership. Whoever owns the customer and the workflow captures the rent. Switching a model is a config change; switching a workflow is a migration.
  • Trust infrastructure. Verification tooling, audit trails, agent identity, and liability insurance for machine action — the businesses that sell certainty in a world of cheap execution.
  • Data gravity. Agents improve fastest inside domains with proprietary, permissioned data — real interactions, real operational histories. Every action generates the training signal for the next one.
Be skeptical of

The fragile middle

  • Thin wrappers. A chat UI over someone else’s model, with no distribution and no proprietary data, is a feature waiting to be absorbed by the platform it depends on.
  • Automation theatre. Demos where the agent works are cheap; deployments where it is trusted are expensive. Value the second, not the first.

The historical pattern holds: in every platform shift, the durable fortunes went to those who owned the scarce complement, not the abundant core. Intelligence is the abundant core here; distribution, trust, and data are the scarce complements.

06

Where the argument bends

A measured take, not a prediction: the agent economy is real infrastructure in the making, but its shape will be decided by forces slower than technology. Forces like these:

Open questions & honest limits — read these before citing

  • Capability is not adoption. Working technology can still take a decade to deploy, because organisations, contracts, and habits move slower than code. The binding constraints are liability law and organisational trust, not benchmarks.
  • The verifier’s regress. If checking an agent’s work requires another agent, who checks the checker? At some point a human signs — and that signature does not scale with compute.
  • Concentration risk. If a handful of providers intermediate most knowledge work, the economy gains a new single point of failure — and a new tollbooth. The diversification logic of the earlier briefs in this series applies here too.
  • No numbers were harmed. This brief deliberately cites no statistics, company anecdotes, or market-size estimates. The argument is structural — which way the pressures point, not how fast they arrive.

The economy that emerges will not be “agents replace everyone.” It will be messier: humans moving up the stack into judgment, specification, and liability-bearing roles — the parts of work where being wrong is expensive. The question is not whether agents do more of the doing. It is who owns the checking, the deciding, and the paying when things go wrong. That is where the value has always been; the agent economy only makes it visible.
