Ground Truth: Bringing SEC EDGAR Into QuantTrade

QuantTrade already had prices, a Monte Carlo engine, and an LLM. What it didn’t have was the company’s own words, filed under oath. On 31 October 2026 I wired in SEC EDGAR — here’s why, what it took, and what it changed.

01

Why filings, when prices were already flowing

A stock price tells you what the market thinks. A filing tells you what the company is legally required to say. Those are very different kinds of truth, and my app only had one of them.

Before this change, QuantTrade’s analysis stood on two legs: market data from financial data APIs, and Google Gemini’s analysis layered on top. That combination describes momentum well. It is weaker at answering the simplest investor question — what does this company actually look like on paper? Most readily available answers are second-hand: summaries of summaries, numbers detached from the documents they came from.

SEC EDGAR is the opposite of that. It is the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission’s public database of company filings — the reports companies themselves submit: annual and quarterly reports, and the other disclosures they are legally accountable for. If a number matters, it is in a filing, in the company’s own words, with a date on it. For an app whose whole job is to inform, going to the primary source felt less like a feature and more like fixing an omission.

Prices are opinions. Filings are testimony. A forecasting tool should hear both before it speaks.

02

What I actually integrated

The integration lives in QuantTrade’s Express / Node.js backend. When a forecast is requested, the backend first resolves the company in EDGAR’s world — a ticker is a market convention, but EDGAR organises everything by the filing company itself — and retrieves its recent filings data.

That filings data then joins the inputs the app already used. Market data still drives the price history behind the Monte Carlo simulation. The difference is context: the filing record travels with the market data into the analysis layer, so when Gemini produces its market analysis, it reasons over what the company has officially reported as well as what the market has been doing. The front end didn’t need to change its shape — a forecast still arrives as a forecast — but what stands behind it is now considerably better sourced.

One design decision mattered more than the rest: EDGAR is treated as a source to visit politely and cache aggressively, not a database to lean on. Filings don’t change by the second, so there is no reason to fetch them by the second either.

03

Engineering lessons from a public API

Building on a free, public, government-run API is a different discipline from building on a commercial one. Nobody sends you a bill — the etiquette is the contract.

Be a good citizen, on purpose. EDGAR is a shared public resource with fair-access expectations. The integration declares itself honestly, throttles its requests, and never retries in a hot loop when something fails. With a commercial API, overuse costs money; with a public one, overuse costs everyone.

Cache like the data deserves it. Filings are published documents; once filed, a document does not change. So caching filing data isn’t an optimisation — it’s the correct model of the world. Repeat forecasts for the same company reuse the same filing record instead of re-fetching it, keeping the app responsive and needless traffic off a public system.

Normalizing is the real work. Getting data back from EDGAR is the easy half. The harder half is that filings are documents written by thousands of different companies, in their own structures and their own words, across different periods. My Monte Carlo engine and the analysis layer expect one consistent shape — so the integration’s centre of gravity is a normalization step: whatever the filing record looks like when it arrives, it leaves as the same predictable structure every other part of the app understands. When that boundary is clean, nothing downstream needs to know EDGAR exists.

TypeScript earns its keep at the boundary. With TypeScript on both sides, the normalized filing shape is a typed contract, not a hope — if the shape changes, the compiler complains before a user sees a broken forecast.

04

What it changed in the output

The forecasts themselves still come from the same place: Monte Carlo simulation over market data, presented as a distribution of outcomes rather than a single guessed number. What changed is the quality of the analysis wrapped around those numbers.

An analysis grounded only in price action can tell you a stock has been falling. One that can also see the company’s filed record can put that fall next to what the company most recently reported about itself — and those two stories agreeing, or pointedly disagreeing, is where the useful signal lives. The Gemini analysis now stands on something firmer: not the internet’s retelling of a company, but the company’s own filings.

It also changed how I trust the app. QuantTrade is an educational tool — it does not give financial advice, and its simulations are not predictions. But “educational” should never mean “casually sourced.” If someone learns from my app’s output, the raw material should come from the most authoritative public source that exists.

The honest limit

Filings are authoritative, but they are also periodic — a company reports on its schedule, not the market’s. EDGAR grounding makes QuantTrade’s analysis better sourced; it does not make any forecast certain, and it doesn’t try to. The Monte Carlo output remains a simulation, and the app remains an educational tool, not financial advice.

05

What’s next

The web app is live; the next step is pockets. QuantTrade’s Android app — Quant Trade Analyzer — has been packaged and signed and submitted to Google Play for closed testing, EDGAR integration included; the release is in review as of Nov 2026. Same engine, same ground truth, smaller screen.
